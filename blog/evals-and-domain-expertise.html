The Role of Domain Expertise in AI Eval Design

July 2026

I build AI systems for a living, mostly in the overlap of language, search, and generated text, where the honest answer to "is this output any good?" is usually "well, it depends." The longer I work on these systems, the more convinced I get that evals, not the model you pick or how clever the prompt is, are what separate something you can trust from a demo built on three examples.

So I went down a bit of a rabbit hole: how do people actually design evals? One question in particular stuck with me. Who gets to decide what "good" even means? For anything in a specialized field, that turns out not to be an engineering call at all. I became more intrigued with the question while working on Juno, a project of mine that turns doctors' notes into plain language, which I'll lean on as the running example. This isn't a definitive guide. I'm not an evals expert, just an engineer sharing what I found interesting on the way down.

What Juno's score measures, and what it misses

Juno already grades everything it produces, with a 0-to-100 Patient Accessibility Score that blends seven readability dimensions (reading grade level, jargon density, and so on), calibrated against health-literacy measures like SMOG and Flesch-Kincaid. It's a solid metric that does exactly what it's meant to: it tells you whether the text is easy to read.

What it can't tell you is whether the text is true. A summary can score comfortably "patient-friendly" and still name a medication the doctor never prescribed, because reading grade level has nothing to say about faithfulness to the source. That isn't a knock on the score; measuring readability was never supposed to catch a wrong dose. It just means readability can't be the only thing standing between the pipeline and the patient. Closing that gap, a summary that's both easy to read and right, is what an eval is for.

Testing it: a seeded-defect experiment

"Readability can't see clinical errors" is easy to assert and more useful to prove, so I ran a small experiment. I took five mock GP consultation notes from the public PriMock57 dataset, ran them through Juno, and planted exactly one defect into a copy of each clean output: a dose multiplied by ten, an invented antibiotic, a dropped follow-up, an urgent warning softened to "keep an eye on this at home," fabricated advice with no numbers in it, and a leaked patient name. Twenty-four broken variants in all, each scored two ways: by the readability composite, and by a set of deterministic checks I call contract graders.

A contract grader is simpler than it sounds. Before the experiment, I wrote down what each note's output had to contain, the way a clinician would when reading it back. For the note that prescribed a three-day nitrofurantoin course, part of that spec looked like this:

"anchors": [
  {"name": "nitrofurantoin",    "critical": true},   // the antibiotic; must survive
  {"name": "3-day course",      "critical": false},
  {"name": "fever or back pain","critical": true}    // safety-net advice; must survive
]

Then a short deterministic check confirms each critical item actually made it into the patient-facing output. No model, no judgment call while it runs, just: did the antibiotic survive? That one flag, "critical": true on nitrofurantoin, is a small piece of a clinician's knowledge written down as something a computer can check. Keep it in mind. Here is how the two scorers did across all 24 variants:

Dose ×1022/2+0.0
Invented medication55/5+0.6
Dropped follow-up21/2−1.5

Invented advice (number-free)50/5+2.4

Two headline numbers, then the one that actually stopped me.

The readability score was blind to all of it. Across 24 broken variants it never moved more than 5 points, in no consistent direction. Fabricated advice actually nudged it up, because "Take an aspirin every day until you feel better" is short, plain, and easy to read. The score isn't broken; it just was never trying to check whether the text was correct.

The contract graders caught 17 of 24, with no LLM involved. Every altered dose, every invented medication, every leaked name, and four of five softened warnings, all from plain rules. The seven misses are the interesting part: the number-free fake advice slipped past every deterministic check, which is exactly the gap an LLM judge is meant to fill next.

And then the one I never planted. On the untouched clean outputs, before any tampering, the anchors grader flagged that one care plan had quietly dropped the prescribed antibiotic. The source note ordered a three-day nitrofurantoin course; the patient-facing version just said to watch for symptoms and call if they got worse. Its readability score was 73: "patient-friendly." A patient following that perfectly readable plan would never take their antibiotic. Five notes, one run, and the check caught exactly the kind of error the readability score is built not to see.

Where domain expertise comes in

Notice what actually did the work there. Not the harness, which is a weekend of code, but the spec, the decision that nitrofurantoin was critical and a clunky sentence is not. That decision isn't an engineering call. It's clinical judgment, and it's the part of an eval you can't buy or infer. The more I looked, the more the expert's job broke into four roles.

Deciding which mistakes matter most. Someone has to rank the failures: a leaked name or an invented drug should block release outright, a dropped follow-up nearly so, an awkward sentence not at all. Every line of that ranking is a statement about patient harm, and it costs an expert almost no time to set.

Writing the gold references. Some qualities, like whether the output stressed the scary warning sign over the trivial one, can only be judged against a handful of expert-written examples. It is the most expensive data in the whole thing, so you spend it only where nothing cheaper works.

Calibrating the judge. An LLM judge is the only way to check open-ended things at scale, but it earns the right to block a release only once it agrees with expert labels often enough. Better still, the experts' time goes into sharpening the criteria the judge applies, not grading outputs one by one.

Keeping the metrics honest. Every automated number is a proxy for a human judgment. When a real reader can answer "which meds, when, which symptoms mean call the doctor?" and the score disagrees, the reader wins and the metric gets fixed.

None of this is unique to me. An ethnographic study of a team building a teaching-advice chatbot found the same shape: their real evaluation work wasn't writing code, it was a series of workshops where domain experts shaped the criteria an LLM judge would later apply. Experts aren't annotation labor. They're the co-authors of what "good" means.

None of this is really about medicine

Swap the clinician for a lawyer, an actuary, or a teacher and the shape holds. The risk tiers become which errors are malpractice versus style. The gold set becomes work an expert would put their name on. The honesty check becomes whatever the real outcome is, cases won or students who learned. An eval built without domain expertise measures what's easy; one built with it measures what matters. And since the eval suite ends up being the most precise description of what a system is actually supposed to do, the experts who shape it aren't consultants to the process. They're its authors.

What's next

This was just the first slice of the design switched on, and it already handed me a to-do list: a dropped antibiotic, a summary that breaks the structure rules, a few empty follow-up sections. Part two is building out the full suite, adding the LLM judges, and then actually using it, tuning Juno's prompts and models with the eval as the referee for whether each change helped. The end state is two layers working together: the readability score making sure what reaches a patient can be understood, and a set of expert-calibrated graders in front of it making sure nothing with a wrong dose or a missing medication reaches them at all. For a tool whose whole value is patients acting on what they read, that second layer isn't a nice-to-have. It's the point.
